The difference between an expensive interior and one that feels truly refined

True luxury isn’t about flawless finishes. It’s about the authenticity of materials, the precision of every detail, and the way a space grows more beautiful with age.

+   Read more−   Close

Natural wood deepens in colour, stone develops character, and metal takes on a patina. Far from diminishing an interior, these subtle changes add depth, personality, and a sense of history.

But there’s an important distinction: beautiful imperfections should be intentional, never an excuse for poor craftsmanship. The more natural and effortless a material appears, the more exacting the architecture, proportions, joinery, and detailing need to be.

This is what makes an interior truly timeless. Rather than following trends that inevitably fade, it develops character and becomes more compelling with every passing year.

The interior you have seen before

A beautiful interior increasingly gives us the feeling that we have seen it before. Familiar colours, striking stone, rounded furniture, carefully arranged objects. Everything looks convincing, yet beneath that harmony it can be difficult to recognise the person who lives there or sense what makes the place distinctive.

+   Read more−   Close

One reason is our shared pool of inspiration. We save similar photographs, study the same projects and choose from a familiar range of solutions. Social media feeds reinforce this cycle: an image we like returns in dozens of variations. Gradually, a familiar aesthetic begins to feel like the only right one, and designing becomes an exercise in reproducing an already approved result.

Even the pursuit of originality can become part of this system. A rare armchair, an unusual light fixture or a vintage table does not, on its own, create individuality. Chosen solely for a striking photograph, these pieces become another collection of recognisable devices. The character of a space emerges from relationships: between objects, architecture, the history of a home and the life of its owner.

This is why designing an interior should begin with getting to know its inhabitants. How do they spend their mornings? Do they enjoy having guests? What do they treasure or collect? Where do they feel at ease? The home itself matters just as much: its proportions, daylight, views and surviving architectural details. These circumstances give a project its own logic and provide reasons for decisions that go deeper than popularity on a mood board.

This approach also allows room for things that do not match perfectly. A painting may sit outside the colour palette, a favourite chair may differ from the rest of the furniture, and books may take up more space than a photograph would ideally allow. These apparent inconsistencies reveal the life within a home. The designer’s task is to make room for them and create a coherent whole that can accommodate change, new interests and new belongings.

An interior’s longevity begins with this same internal logic. Well-considered proportions, a practical layout, thoughtful lighting and materials that age gracefully remain relevant beyond seasonal trends. Fashion can play a part in a project without determining every decision.

Creating distinctive spaces requires curiosity beyond collections of interior images: exploring cities, reading, paying attention to art, learning about craft and listening to people. Each choice then acquires a story of its own. And the interior develops a character that cannot be achieved simply by reproducing a favourite photograph.

Barcelona's hydraulic tiles

The uniqueness and history of Catalan hydraulic tiles (Spanish: mosaico hidráulico) are inseparably linked to Barcelona's architectural heritage, the technical innovations of the 19th century, and the contemporary movement to preserve urban art.

+   Read more−   Close

The technology for manufacturing hydraulic tiles appeared in Europe in the mid-19th century. Unlike traditional ceramics, these tiles do not require firing: they are made from cement, sand, marble dust, and natural pigments, which are compressed under high pressure.

The golden age of this material in Catalonia coincided with the era of Modernisme. Prominent architects of the period, such as Antoni Gaudí, Lluís Domènech i Montaner, and other Catalan master builders, designed unique floor patterns, believing that the flooring should harmonize with facades and furniture to achieve an aesthetic unity throughout the building. By the mid-20th century, the popularity of these tiles declined due to the emergence of lighter and cheaper materials, such as terrazzo and porcelain stoneware.

What makes them unique

According to various sources, the value of Catalan tiles is determined by several factors:

  • Handcrafted production: Each tile is made individually. Using special metal moulds (matrices), artisans create intricate geometric or floral patterns that are impossible to reproduce exactly by industrial means.
  • Democratization of art: Hydraulic tiles made it possible to bring the refined decorative language of Modernisme into the homes of ordinary people who could not afford costly architectural commissions.
  • Incredible diversity: It is estimated that thousands of different designs exist. Even today, collectors discover previously unknown patterns beneath layers of modern cement or laminate flooring.
  • Durability and aesthetics: The use of natural pigments and marble provides deep, rich colours and exceptional wear resistance.

A contemporary revival: the "tile hunters"

In recent years, a phenomenon of "tile hunters" has emerged in Barcelona, with figures such as Joel Canovas (@tilehunter) and Marc Ocaña. They rescue historic floor fragments quite literally from construction debris during renovations of old apartments.

Today, these tiles have evolved from an ordinary building material into a symbol of urban memory and an object of collecting. Specialized venues are opening in Barcelona, such as the "Tile Museum" (El Temple de la Rajola), dedicated to the study, cataloguing, and restoration of this heritage. Contemporary designers are also returning to this material, using it in interiors to imbue spaces with character and uniqueness.

In our projects we treat the historical heritage with great care and, whenever possible, restore such floors with particular attention to detail.
